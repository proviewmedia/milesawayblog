<h2>What a VPN Actually Does</h2>

<p>It builds an encrypted tunnel between your device and a server somewhere else, then sends your traffic out from there. Two consequences follow. The network you are physically on cannot see what you are doing, and the sites you visit see the server's location rather than yours.</p>

<p>That is the whole product. Everything else is a consequence of those two facts.</p>

<h2>The Honest Security Case</h2>

<p>Here is the part most reviews skip. Nearly every site and app you use already encrypts its traffic with HTTPS. Someone sharing a cafe network genuinely cannot read your banking password out of the air the way they might have in 2012. That threat has largely been engineered away.</p>

<p>What remains real is smaller but not nothing:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Evil twin hotspots.</strong> Anyone can name a hotspot "Hotel Guest Wifi" and wait. Connect to the wrong one and your traffic runs through a stranger's equipment. A VPN makes that traffic useless to them.</li>
  <li><strong>The network operator can see where you go.</strong> HTTPS hides the contents of a page, not which sites you visited. Whoever runs the hotel network can build that list.</li>
  <li><strong>Some networks interfere.</strong> Hotel and airport networks sometimes inject content, throttle services or block things arbitrarily. A tunnel routes around all of it.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Do I really need a VPN when travelling?</h3>
<p>It depends on what you do. If you only use your own mobile data and apps like WhatsApp and banking, which are already encrypted, the security benefit is modest. If you regularly work from hotel, cafe and airport wifi, or you want to reach services from home that are geo-restricted abroad, a VPN earns its place. It is useful rather than essential for most travellers.</p>

<h3>Is public wifi actually dangerous?</h3>
<p>Less than it used to be. Nearly all websites and apps now use HTTPS, which already encrypts your traffic, so the classic story of someone reading your passwords in a cafe is largely outdated. The remaining real risks are fake hotspots impersonating a hotel network and having your browsing visible to whoever runs the network. A VPN addresses both.</p>

<h3>Can I use a VPN to watch my home streaming abroad?</h3>
<p>Sometimes, and it is not guaranteed. Streaming services actively detect and block VPN traffic, so a server that works this month may not next month. NordVPN performs better than most here, but treat access as a bonus and not a reason to buy.</p>

<h3>Is a VPN legal?</h3>
<p>In most countries yes, and it is a normal business tool. A handful of countries including China, Russia, Iran and the UAE restrict or ban VPN use to varying degrees. Check the rules for your destination before you travel rather than assuming.</p>

<p>Vancouver is one of those cities that ruins other cities for you. I don't say that lightly — I've traveled a lot, and most places are great for one or two specific reasons. Vancouver is great for almost every reason at once: mountains and ocean in the same frame, one of the most diverse food scenes in North America, and a walkable, bikeable downtown wrapped in a 1,000-acre rainforest. This is my complete, been-there guide to visiting Vancouver, British Columbia — when to go, how to get around, the best things to do, and where to eat.</p>
<p>[banner]</p>

<p>Here's what puts it in perspective: Vancouver is the only major city in North America where you can ski a world-class mountain in the morning, kayak a sheltered ocean bay in the afternoon, and eat your way through night markets by evening — all without renting a car. That's not a brochure line. I did exactly that in a single January day.</p>

<h2>Best Time to Visit Vancouver</h2>
<p>The best time to visit Vancouver is <strong>June through September</strong>, when the city trades its rainy reputation for long, golden, low-70s days with almost zero humidity. July and August are peak season — the warmest weather and every patio open, but also the biggest crowds and highest hotel prices.</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>June–September (summer):</strong> The sweet spot. Warm, dry, and daylight until nearly 10pm. Book hotels early.</li>
  <li><strong>April–May &amp; October (shoulder season):</strong> Fewer crowds and lower prices, with cherry blossoms in spring and fall color in October. Pack a light rain jacket.</li>
  <li><strong>November–March (winter):</strong> Rainy and mild in the city, but this is ski season — Whistler is two hours away and the local mountains are 30 minutes from downtown.</li>
</ul>
<p>Vancouver's rain reputation is worse than the reality. The wet stretch is fall and winter, and even then it's usually a soft, persistent drizzle rather than a dramatic downpour.</p>

<h2>How to Get to Vancouver (and Get Around)</h2>
<p>Vancouver International Airport (YVR) is one of the best-connected airports on the West Coast, with direct flights from most major U.S. and international hubs. From the airport, skip the taxi line: the <strong>Canada Line SkyTrain</strong> gets you from YVR to downtown in about 26 minutes for roughly CA$9 — one of the best airport-transit deals anywhere.</p>
<p>Once you're in the city, you almost certainly don't need a car. The SkyTrain, buses, and the SeaBus ferry cover everywhere a visitor wants to go, and downtown, Stanley Park, and the beaches are genuinely walkable and bikeable. Rent a car only if you're heading to Whistler, the Fraser Valley, or up the Sea-to-Sky Highway — which you should, because it's one of the most beautiful drives in the country.</p>

<h2>The Best Things to Do in Vancouver</h2>
<p>You could spend a week here and not run out of things to do. These are the experiences I'd put at the top of any first-timer's Vancouver itinerary.</p>

<h3>Stanley Park &amp; the Seawall</h3>
<p>Stanley Park is the reason Vancouver feels different from every other city. It's a 1,000-acre old-growth forest attached to downtown — thick Douglas fir and cedar, rocky coastline, and a paved <strong>Seawall trail</strong> that rings the whole thing. Walk or bike the loop (about 9 km, 90 minutes on foot) and you'll pass beaches, the famous totem poles, Lions Gate Bridge, and a skyline-and-mountains view that recalibrates what you think a city can be. Rent a bike near the entrance and ride counter-clockwise — it's the required direction, and it keeps the ocean on your side.</p>

<h3>Granville Island Public Market</h3>
<p>Granville Island is a former industrial peninsula turned food-and-arts district, and its <strong>Public Market</strong> is the heart of it: Dungeness crab pulled from the boat that morning, artisan cheese, Okanagan fruit, and bakers who take their craft seriously. Get there before 10am — it fills up fast and the good stuff sells out. Grab lunch, wander the artisan studios, then catch one of the tiny rainbow ferries back across False Creek.</p>

<blockquote><p>"Vancouver is the only major city where you can ski a mountain in the morning, kayak the ocean in the afternoon, and hit a night market by evening."</p></blockquote>

<h3>Capilano Suspension Bridge &amp; Grouse Mountain</h3>
<p>Fifteen minutes north of downtown, the mountains start. The <strong>Capilano Suspension Bridge</strong> sways 70 meters above a rainforest canyon and connects to a network of cliff-walks and treetop bridges. Just up the road, <strong>Grouse Mountain</strong> runs a gondola to sweeping city views, and in winter it becomes a ski hill you can see from downtown. Do both in a half-day.</p>

<h3>Gastown &amp; Historic Downtown</h3>
<p>Gastown is Vancouver's oldest neighborhood — cobblestone streets, Victorian brick, the steam-powered clock everyone photographs, and some of the city's best restaurants and cocktail bars packed into a few walkable blocks. It's the perfect place to end a day.</p>

<h2>Where to Eat: Vancouver's Food Scene</h2>
<p>Vancouver's food is shaped by geography and immigration in equal measure. The city has one of the largest Asian populations of any city in North America, and the result is a dining scene that punches far above its size.</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Richmond's night markets &amp; Chinese food:</strong> The suburb of Richmond, 20 minutes south, is regularly called the best Chinese-food destination outside of Asia. It's worth a dedicated trip.</li>
  <li><strong>Fresh seafood:</strong> Spot prawns, Dungeness crab, wild salmon, and oysters, often landed the same day.</li>
  <li><strong>Japanese &amp; izakaya:</strong> Some of the best sushi and izakaya dining outside Japan.</li>
  <li><strong>Food trucks:</strong> Downtown's street-food scene, especially around the art gallery, is excellent and budget-friendly.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Where to Stay in Vancouver</h2>
<p>Where you stay shapes the whole trip. Quick neighborhood guide:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Downtown / Coal Harbour:</strong> Best for first-timers — walk to Stanley Park, the waterfront, and Gastown.</li>
  <li><strong>Yaletown:</strong> Trendy and walkable, with great restaurants and nightlife along False Creek.</li>
  <li><strong>Kitsilano ("Kits"):</strong> Laid-back beach vibe, indie shops, and the best sunset beach in the city.</li>
  <li><strong>West End:</strong> Quieter and leafy, steps from both the park and the beaches — often better value.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What Vancouver Costs</h2>
<p>Vancouver isn't cheap, but it's manageable with a little planning. Expect mid-range hotels around CA$200–350/night in summer, transit passes around CA$11/day, and restaurant mains from CA$20–35. The most memorable things — Stanley Park, the Seawall, the beaches, and the views — are completely free.</p>

<h2>Vancouver Travel FAQ</h2>
<p><strong>How many days do you need in Vancouver?</strong><br>Three to four days is the sweet spot for a first visit — enough for Stanley Park, Granville Island, the mountains, and the food, with a day left for a Sea-to-Sky or Whistler day trip.</p>
<p><strong>Is Vancouver expensive to visit?</strong><br>It's one of Canada's pricier cities for hotels, but transit is cheap, and its best experiences — the Seawall, beaches, and mountain views — are free. Shoulder season (spring and fall) saves the most.</p>
<p><strong>Do you need a car in Vancouver?</strong><br>No. The SkyTrain and buses cover the city and downtown is walkable. Rent a car only for day trips to Whistler or the Sea-to-Sky Highway.</p>
<p><strong>What is the best time to visit Vancouver?</strong><br>June through September, for warm, dry, low-70s days and long daylight. Spring and fall are quieter and cheaper; winter is rainy in the city but great for nearby skiing.</p>

<p>Vancouver will change your benchmark for what a city should feel like. Go once and you'll understand why it ruins other cities — in the best possible way.</p>
